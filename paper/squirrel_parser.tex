\title[The Squirrel Parser]{The Squirrel Parser: Packrat Parsing with Left Recursion and Minimum-Cost Error Recovery}

\author{Luke A. D. Hutchison}
\email{luke.hutch@alum.mit.edu}
\orcid{0000-0002-2937-8619}

\begin{abstract}
The \emph{squirrel parsing algorithm} extends packrat parsing to directly support all forms of left recursion: direct, indirect, input-dependent, and interwoven left-recursive cycles, to any degree of complexity. The extension consists of adding three metadata fields to each memo table entry and an expansion loop to the memo-table access function, and it reduces exactly to standard packrat parsing whenever no left-recursive cycle is encountered, so its cost on non-left-recursive grammars is negligible. For left-recursive grammars, parsing time remains linear in the length of the input whenever the expansion regions of left-recursive cycles are disjoint, a condition satisfied by every practical grammar we examined; we exhibit an adversarial grammar, nesting syntactic lookahead over a left-recursive rule, for which the algorithm (in common with prior bounded-left-recursion approaches) requires quadratic time. This paper also introduces an error recovery algorithm for PEG parsing that keeps the parser intact and runs the same recursive descent a second time in a costed form: every clause returns a set of candidate \emph{readings} of the input, each pricing the characters it deletes as noise and the required constructs it records as missing, and for each clause, position, and end position only the best-priced reading survives. The search deepens a repair budget one edit at a time and stops at the first budget at which some reading explains the entire input, so recovery returns a complete parse tree over the unmodified input, with damage confined to explicit syntax-error marks, at minimum cost under a stated seven-part comparison. The recovery never invents characters: text the grammar requires but the input lacks is reported as missing, never spelled, which is also what keeps the objective tractable, since we prove that exact minimum-distance repair with invented text is NP-hard for PEGs, and that any efficient compositional repairer must carry set-valued summaries. Left recursion is handled inside recovery by the same seed-growing mechanism the parser itself uses. Over an exhaustive mutation battery of 2{,}101 damaged documents across three grammars, including a left-recursive expression grammar, recovery restored the exact tree skeleton a reader of the undamaged document would expect in 85.8\% of cases, with a mean skeleton similarity of 0.990, at about a third of a millisecond per document; valid inputs pay nothing, because the plain parse is the search's own zeroth round.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The earliest described parsing algorithms, used to parse programs written in ALGOL~60~\cite{irons1961syntax,lucas1961structure}, were recursive algorithms that worked top-down. Despite the many parsing algorithms developed since~\cite{kegler2023parsing}, recursive descent parsing has remained popular, because it is simple to implement, and because the structure of a recursive descent parser's code directly mirrors the structure of the underlying grammar. Recursive descent parsers have, however, suffered from three longstanding drawbacks: in their direct formulations they repeat work, and can require exponential time when backtracking; they cannot parse left-recursive grammars, because a left-recursive rule calls itself before consuming any input, triggering infinite recursion; and they recover poorly from syntax errors, typically reporting a single failure position and stopping.

The first drawback has been solved. Norvig showed that memoizing a top-down parser eliminates its repeated work~\cite{norvig1991}, and Ford developed this observation into \emph{packrat parsing}, a memoizing recursive descent algorithm that parses in time linear in the length of the input~\cite{ford2002,ford2002b}, building on top-down backtracking recognition schemes studied three decades earlier by Birman and Ullman~\cite{birman1970,birman1973}. Ford subsequently introduced \emph{parsing expression grammars} (PEGs) as a recognition-based formalism for specifying the grammars that packrat parsers accept~\cite{ford2004}. In contrast to the generative formalisms that preceded them, PEG rules denote greedily recognized patterns, and the PEG choice operator is ordered, replacing the ambiguity of context-free grammars with deterministic choice, so that a PEG grammar yields exactly one parse of any input it accepts.

The second and third drawbacks have both remained open, in the specific sense that no known solution preserves the properties that make packrat parsing attractive in the first place: implementation simplicity, unmodified grammars, faithful parse tree structure, and linear-time performance.

For left recursion, the available workarounds each give up one of those properties. Rewriting a left-recursive grammar into non-left-recursive form, whether manually or automatically, changes the shape of the resulting parse tree, so the semantic structure of the grammar is no longer directly represented in the structure of the parse, and postprocessing is needed to recover it. ANTLR handles direct left recursion by exactly such an internal rewriting step, and does not accept indirect left recursion~\cite{parr2014}. Warth et al.\ modified the packrat memoization mechanism to grow left-recursive matches iteratively from a seed~\cite{warth2008}, but the resulting algorithm requires substantial bookkeeping beyond the memo table (recursion heads, involved-rule sets, and a rule invocation stack), and Tratt later showed that its treatment of some grammars is surprising or incorrect, proposing a restricted alternative that handles direct left recursion only~\cite{tratt2010}. Medeiros et al.\ gave a formal semantics for left-recursive PEGs in terms of bounded left recursion, in which a left-recursive rule is re-evaluated under successively larger recursion bounds until its match length stops growing~\cite{medeiros2014}. Frost et al.\ handle left recursion in parser combinators by curtailing recursion depth against the remaining input length~\cite{frost2008}. These approaches collectively established that left-recursive top-down parsing is possible, but each is either restricted in the forms of left recursion it accepts, or requires machinery whose complexity has discouraged adoption.

For error recovery, the situation is starker: fifty years of work on syntax error recovery has produced practical methods for LR and LL table-driven parsers, yet almost none of it transfers to recursive descent parsers in general or to PEG parsers in particular, for structural reasons. Aho and Peterson showed in 1972 that a context-free parser can be made globally error-correcting, producing a parse whose distance from the input is minimal, by extending the grammar with error productions and parsing with an Earley-style algorithm in cubic time~\cite{aho1972}; Lyon gave a practical variant~\cite{lyon1974}. The minimum-distance objective was thereafter largely abandoned as impractical, in favor of local repair heuristics: Burke and Fisher's method considers single-token repairs at the failure point, validated by parsing ahead a fixed number of tokens~\cite{burke1987}, and the recent CPCT+ algorithm of Diekmann and Tratt computes complete sets of minimum-cost repair sequences at the failure point of an LR parser~\cite{diekmann2020}. All of these operate on the parser's internal configuration at the moment of failure, and therefore cannot repair an error whose edit lies \emph{before} the failure point, as an unclosed string literal requires. In the PEG setting, proposed recovery mechanisms attach recovery expressions to labeled failure points in the grammar~\cite{medeiros2018,medeiros2020}, which requires per-grammar recovery engineering; in the scannerless generalized-LR setting, de Jonge et al.\ derive permissive grammars with recovery productions automatically~\cite{dejonge2012}. The pika parser, our earlier work, inverts packrat parsing into a right-to-left dynamic programming algorithm, which handles left recursion and supports recovery after syntax errors, at a significant constant-factor performance cost, and with recovery anchored to user-designated recovery rules~\cite{hutchison2020}.

Both problems, we contend, yield to the same discipline: identify the invariants that make the packrat architecture work, and extend the architecture only in ways that preserve them. For left recursion, the load-bearing invariant is the purity of memoization, and re-examining what a left-recursive cycle means to a memoizing parser leads to an algorithm, the \emph{squirrel parser}, that handles every form of left recursion by adding three metadata fields to each memo entry and one expansion loop to the memo access function, reducing exactly to packrat parsing whenever no cycle is met. For error recovery, the load-bearing invariant is the parser itself: prior methods modify the parser, or the grammar, to tolerate errors, and the modification is what cascades. Keeping the plain parser intact, and running the same descent a second time in a form where failure has a price instead of being final, turns recovery into a bounded search over competing whole-document readings whose answer is declarative: a complete parse of the unmodified input at minimum repair cost, with every deleted character and every missing construct marked in the tree, in place of a collection of heuristics.

\subsection{Parsing expression grammars}
\label{sec:peg_grammars}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A PEG grammar consists of a finite set of \emph{rules}. Each rule has the form $A \leftarrow e$, where $A$ is a unique \emph{rule name}, and $e$ is a \emph{parsing expression}, also referred to as a \emph{clause}. One rule is designated as the \emph{top rule}, which becomes the root for top-down recursive parsing. Clauses are constructed from \emph{subclauses}, \emph{terminals}, and \emph{rule references}. The subclauses within a rule's clause form a tree, with \emph{PEG operators} at the non-leaf nodes, and terminals or rule references at the leaf nodes.

There are four principal PEG operators (Table~\ref{tab:peg_operators}): \cd{Seq} advances the parser through a series of subclause matches; \cd{First} (ordered choice) matches as soon as one of its subclauses, tried in order, matches; \cd{OneOrMore} matches a sequence of one or more instances of its subclause; and \cd{NotFollowedBy} is a lookahead operator that consumes no input, and matches only if its subclause does not match at the current position. Three further operators can be implemented directly or derived from the first four: \cd{Optional} matches zero or one instances of its subclause; \cd{ZeroOrMore} matches zero or more instances; and \cd{FollowedBy} is a lookahead operator that matches only if its subclause matches at the current position, without consuming any input. Terminals match individual characters, strings, or character classes. One special terminal is the empty string $\epsilon$, which matches at any position, even beyond the end of the input, and consumes zero characters.

A clause matches at a given input position, or fails to match there; when it matches, it consumes zero or more characters, and the parsing position advances by the length of the match. We write $n$ for the length of the input, $|G|$ for the number of rules in the grammar, and $L(G)$ for the language of the grammar: the set of strings that the top rule matches in their entirety. A \emph{parser state} is a pair $(R, p)$ of a rule and an input position, $p \in [0, n]$; position $n$, one past the last character, is a legal match position because lookaheads and $\epsilon$ may be evaluated there.

All non-left-recursive PEG grammars can be parsed directly by a recursive descent parser, and can be parsed in linear time by a packrat parser. PEGs are strictly more powerful than regular expressions, and can express some non-context-free languages, but it is conjectured, and still open, that some context-free languages cannot be recognized by any PEG~\cite{ford2004}. A PEG parser inherits the limitations of other recursive descent parsers: without special handling it cannot parse left-recursive grammars, and it reports errors poorly.

Throughout the paper, grammars are written in the ASCII notation of the reference implementation: \cd{A <- e ;} defines a rule, \cd{'x'} and \cd{"abc"} are literal terminals, \cd{[0-9]} is a character class, and the operators are as in Table~\ref{tab:peg_operators}.

\section{Deconstructing left recursion}
\label{sec:deconstruct}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The only state visible to a recursion frame in a recursive descent parser, setting aside the memo table for the moment, is the parser state $(R, p)$: the rule being matched, and the input position at which it is being matched. If the same state is visited twice within one recursion path, then zero characters were consumed between the two visits, since $p$ is the same both times, and nothing else distinguishes the two frames. The parser will therefore repeat the same descent from the second visit that it began from the first, and recurse forever. A cycle in the grammar is \emph{left recursive}, for a given input, exactly when the parser can traverse the whole cycle without consuming a character. Conversely, if every loop around a cycle consumes at least one character, the parser makes monotonic progress toward the end of the finite input, the recursion depth is bounded by a linear function of the input length, and the cycle is harmless: this is why an ordinary recursive descent parser handles so-called right-recursive rules such as $E \leftarrow N \; \texttt{'+'} \; E \; / \; N$ without difficulty. The distinction between left recursion and non-left recursion is not which operand position refers back into the cycle, but whether the parser must consume input before it can re-enter the cycle.

\begin{lemma}[Monotonic progress]
\label{lem:monotonic}
A recursive descent parser terminates on a grammar cycle if and only if each loop around the cycle consumes at least one character of the input. Consequently, the only consistent interpretation of a left-recursive cycle, other than infinite recursion, requires each successive expansion of the cycle to consume at least one additional character.
\end{lemma}

For parse tree nodes corresponding to matches of a left-recursive rule, Lemma~\ref{lem:monotonic} means that each successively higher node in the parse tree must match at least one more character of the input than the node below it.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Parse trees are built bottom-up}
\label{sec:postorder}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

It is a common belief that a recursive descent parser builds the parse tree from the top down, and this belief makes left recursion look unresolvable, since the parser would have to know how tall the left-recursive subtree will be before descending into it. But a recursive descent parser does not create the parse tree node for the current recursion frame until all requisite descendant clauses have been found to match the input: a clause is determined to match only after its subclauses have been matched, and the node is created on \emph{exit} from the frame. Recursive descent is a postorder traversal of the grammar with respect to the input, and a postorder traversal builds the tree on the way back up. In this respect top-down and bottom-up parsers both build the parse tree from the bottom up, which makes intuitive sense: the larger-scale structure of the input cannot be known until its finer-grained structure has been determined.

There is also a stronger reason to handle left recursion bottom-up. It is not possible to know \emph{a priori} how many times to recurse around a left-recursive cycle before terminating the descent: the number of loops that will match is a property of the input, discoverable only by attempting successively larger matches until a larger match can no longer be found. Lemma~\ref{lem:monotonic} requires each loop to consume at least one additional character, but does not say how many more than one, and the number of characters a given loop consumes cannot be known until the number consumed by the next-lower loop is known.

\begin{lemma}[Bottom-up expansion]
\label{lem:bottomup}
A left-recursive cycle must be matched against the input bottom-up, by attempting successively taller stacks of loops around the cycle, because the height at which the cycle stops matching is discoverable only from below.
\end{lemma}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The fixed point of a left-recursive cycle}
\label{sec:fixedpoint}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Under Lemma~\ref{lem:monotonic}, consider the deepest frames of a descent into a left-recursive cycle. Any higher recursion frame in the same recursive path must match monotonically more characters than a lower frame, the input is finite, and in the extreme case the topmost frame for the cycle matches the entire input; so each successively lower frame has strictly less input available to it, and after finitely many frames the rule can no longer match at all. We term this bottom-most mismatch the \emph{fixed point} of the cycle. The fixed point seeds the bottom-up expansion of Lemma~\ref{lem:bottomup}: the first expansion attempt matches the cycle's non-recursive alternative (if any) above the seeded mismatch, and each subsequent attempt may incorporate the previous, shorter match as a subtree of a taller, longer match.

Expansion cannot continue forever, again because the input is finite, so a stopping criterion is needed, and Lemma~\ref{lem:monotonic} supplies it: expansion stops as soon as a successively longer match can no longer be found. In a memoizing parser this criterion takes the form of two update rules for the memo table: a match may only ever be replaced by a longer match, and a match may never be replaced by a mismatch. The intent of a grammar author writing a left-recursive rule is for the rule to match as much of the input as it can at its start position, so the last and longest match found becomes the match for the cycle.

\begin{lemma}[Fixed point and stopping criterion]
\label{lem:fixpoint}
Descent into a left-recursive cycle terminates at a mismatch, the fixed point, from which the cycle is expanded bottom-up; expansion terminates when the match length stops increasing, and the longest match found is the match for the cycle.
\end{lemma}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Direct, indirect, and input-dependent left recursion}
\label{sec:lrforms}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Much of the literature distinguishes \emph{direct} left recursion, where a rule refers to itself in leftmost position, from \emph{indirect} left recursion, where the cycle passes through other rules, and several algorithms handle only the direct form~\cite{tratt2010,parr2014}. From the standpoint of the analysis above, the distinction is immaterial: what classifies a path through the grammar as left recursive is whether a loop around the cycle can consume zero characters, and the length of the cycle plays no role in that criterion, in the detection of a repeated parser state, or in bottom-up expansion from the fixed point.

Nor is left recursion a static property of a rule. Whether a given cycle causes the parser to re-enter the same parser state depends on the input. The rule $A \leftarrow \texttt{'x'?} \; (A \; \texttt{'y'} \; / \; A \; / \; \texttt{'y'})$ recurses left recursively exactly when \cd{'x'} fails to match at the current position, since \cd{'x'?} then consumes zero characters; and in the grammar $A \leftarrow B \; / \; \texttt{'z'};\; B \leftarrow (\texttt{'x'} \; A) \; / \; (A \; \texttt{'y'})$, the cycle through $B$'s second alternative is entered only where the input diverts matching away from the first alternative. A parser therefore cannot soundly decide where to apply left recursion handling by inspecting the grammar alone.

\begin{lemma}[Dynamic detection]
\label{lem:dynamic}
Left recursion handling must be triggered by detecting a repeated parser state $(R, p)$ during parsing, not by static analysis of the grammar, and must treat cycles of every length identically.
\end{lemma}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Algorithm outline}
\label{sec:sketch}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Lemmas \ref{lem:monotonic} through \ref{lem:dynamic} together determine the shape of a left-recursion-capable memoizing recursive descent parser:

\begin{itemize}
    \item On entering a recursion frame to match rule $R$ at position $p$, determine whether the parser state $(R, p)$ was already visited in a higher frame of the current recursion path.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item If not, proceed with standard memoized recursive descent.
        \item If so, this frame is the fixed point of a left-recursive cycle (Lemma~\ref{lem:fixpoint}): record a mismatch for $(R, p)$, and signal to the ancestral frame with the same parser state that it must expand the cycle bottom-up.
    \end{itemize}
    \item If the current frame was signaled, iteratively re-match the same rule at the same position until the match no longer grows (Lemma~\ref{lem:fixpoint}).
    \item Update the memo entry for $(R, p)$ only when a match grows longer; never replace a match with a mismatch.
\end{itemize}

The ideas behind bottom-up growth of a left-recursive match are shared with the seed-growing algorithm of Warth et al.~\cite{warth2008} and with the bounded left recursion semantics of Medeiros et al.~\cite{medeiros2014}. What has been missing is a formulation that is simple, uniform across all of the cases of Lemma~\ref{lem:dynamic}, and correct: Warth et al.'s algorithm requires auxiliary data structures beyond the memo table (a rule invocation stack, recursion ``heads,'' and per-head involved-rule sets), and both Tratt and Medeiros et al.\ report grammars for which it produces incorrect or surprising parses~\cite{tratt2010,medeiros2014}; Tratt's own alternative deliberately restricts itself to direct left recursion~\cite{tratt2010}; and Medeiros et al.'s semantics re-evaluates the rule under an explicit increasing bound. The algorithm presented next implements the outline with three metadata fields added to each memo entry and no other data structures, handling direct and indirect left recursion identically per Lemma~\ref{lem:dynamic}. We term it the \emph{squirrel parser}.

\section{The squirrel parsing algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Fig.~\ref{fig:Code} gives implementation-level pseudocode for the complete algorithm (left), and for representative PEG clause types (right).

The pseudocode style was chosen to be unambiguous but generically readable by anyone familiar with a C-like object-oriented language, which we prefer to inventing a new abstract pseudocode notation. Classes are declared with the keyword \cd{class}, followed by an optional parenthesized list of fields set by an implicit constructor, an optional superclass after a colon, and the class body. Memory allocation with \cd{new} zero-initializes all fields and array elements, storing zero, \cd{null}, or \cd{false} as appropriate. \cd{[]} denotes the empty array, and \cd{[m]} an array containing the single element \cd{m}. Standard C syntax is used for local variables, loops, and pre-increment (\cd{++x}). The \cd{MemoTable} class (not shown) has a single method \cd{getOrCreateEntry(rule, pos)}, which returns the \cd{MemoEntry} stored under the key $(R, p)$, first creating and storing a new zero-initialized entry if none exists; it may be implemented as a dense two-dimensional array indexed by rule and position, which is faster, or as a hashmap keyed by the pair, which uses less memory. The clause types not shown follow directly from the PEG operator definitions of Table~\ref{tab:peg_operators}, with one caveat: \cd{OneOrMore} and \cd{ZeroOrMore} must stop iterating if their subclause yields a zero-length match, since otherwise the empty match would be consumed forever, the iterative counterpart of an unhandled left-recursive cycle.

class Match(Clause clause, int pos, int len,
            Match[] subClauseMatches);


const Match MISMATCH =
    new Match(null, -1, -1, []);


class Parser(Clause topRule) {
  String input;
  MemoTable memoTable;
  int[] memoVersionForPos;

  Match match(Clause rule, int pos) {
    var memoEntry =
        memoTable.getOrCreateEntry(rule, pos);
    return memoEntry.match(this, rule, pos);
  }

  Match parse(String inputStr) {
    input = inputStr;
    memoVersionForPos =
        new int[input.length + 1];
    memoTable = new MemoTable();
    return match(topRule, 0);
  }
}


class MemoEntry {
  Match match;
  boolean inRecPath;
  boolean foundLeftRec;
  int memoVersion;

  Match match(Parser parser, Clause rule,
              int pos) {
    if (match == null || memoVersion
        < parser.memoVersionForPos[pos]) {
      if (inRecPath) {
        if (match == null) {
          foundLeftRec = true;
          match = MISMATCH;
        }
      } else {
        inRecPath = true;
        while (true) {
          var newMatch = rule.match(parser, pos);
          if (match != null
              && newMatch.len <= match.len)
            break;
          match = newMatch;
          if (!foundLeftRec)
            break;
          memoVersion =
              ++(parser.memoVersionForPos[pos]);
        }
        inRecPath = false;
      }
      memoVersion = parser.memoVersionForPos[pos];
    }
    return match;
  }
}

abstract class Clause {
  abstract Match match(Parser parser, int pos);
}


class First(Clause[] subClauses) : Clause {
  Match match(Parser parser, int pos) {
    for (var i = 0;
         i < subClauses.length; i++) {
      var m = subClauses[i].match(parser, pos);
      if (m != MISMATCH) {
        return new Match(this, pos, m.len, [m]);
      }
    }
    return MISMATCH;
  }
}


class Seq(Clause[] subClauses) : Clause {
  Match match(Parser parser, int pos) {
    var ma = new Match[subClauses.length];
    var p = pos;
    for (var i = 0;
         i < subClauses.length; i++) {
      var m = subClauses[i].match(parser, p);
      if (m == MISMATCH) {
        return MISMATCH;
      }
      ma[i] = m;
      p += m.len;
    }
    return new Match(this, pos, p - pos, ma);
  }
}


class Char(char c) : Clause {
  Match match(Parser parser, int pos) {
    if (pos < parser.input.length
        && parser.input[pos] == c) {
      return new Match(this, pos, 1, []);
    }
    return MISMATCH;
  }
}


class RuleRef(String refdRuleName,
              Clause refdRule) : Clause {
  Match match(Parser parser, int pos) {
    var m = parser.match(refdRule, pos);
    if (m == MISMATCH) {
      return MISMATCH;
    }
    return new Match(this, pos, m.len, [m]);
  }
}

In this implementation, each rule of the grammar is a tree of clauses, and the rule is represented by a reference to the topmost clause of that tree. For example, the rule $A \leftarrow (B \; / \; C) \; \texttt{'x'}$ has the name $A$ and consists of the clause tree \cd{Seq(First(RuleRef("B"), RuleRef("C")), Char('x'))}, with the \cd{Seq} clause reference standing for the whole rule. The result of matching the top clause of a rule at a given position is memoized; the results of matching the interior clauses of the tree are deliberately not.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Walk-through}
\label{sec:walkthrough}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The code in Fig.~\ref{fig:Code} is short and appears simple, but there is a great deal of nuance in how it operates. The key trick at the heart of the algorithm is to store metadata about the current recursion path, and about efforts to expand left-recursive cycles, directly in the memo entries, whether or not a memo entry yet holds a match result. Because the descendant frame that detects a cycle and the ancestral frame that must expand it share the same \cd{MemoEntry} object, the memo table itself becomes the communication channel between frames, and the auxiliary data structures of comparable algorithms (Section~\ref{sec:sketch}) disappear into it.

\cd{Match} objects form the parse tree: each records the clause that matched, the start position and length of the match, and the matches of the subclauses that constitute it. The singleton \cd{MISMATCH} represents a failed match attempt. Its \cd{len} field is the sentinel $-1$, so that a match of any length, including a zero-length match of $\epsilon$, compares longer than a mismatch; the comparison \cd{newMatch.len <= match.len} in \cd{MemoEntry.match} therefore implements both memo update rules of Lemma~\ref{lem:fixpoint} at once, allowing a mismatch to be replaced by any match, a shorter match by a longer one, and never a match by a mismatch.

The \cd{Parser} class holds the input, the memo table, and the array \cd{memoVersionForPos}, which counts how many times left-recursive expansion has been performed at each input position. Since match attempts may be made at position $n$, one past the end of the input (Section~\ref{sec:peg_grammars}), the array is allocated with $n + 1$ entries. \cd{Parser.match} looks up or creates the memo entry for $(R, p)$ and delegates to it; \cd{parse} starts the process by matching the top rule at position 0.

Each call to \cd{MemoEntry.match} represents one rule-level recursion frame. Its behavior is governed by the entry's four fields:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \cd{match} stores the best (longest) match found so far for this rule at this position, or \cd{null} before any attempt has completed, or \cd{MISMATCH}.

    \item \cd{inRecPath} is true while an attempt to match this entry's rule is anywhere on the current recursion path: it is set before recursing into the rule's clause tree and cleared on exit. If \cd{MemoEntry.match} is entered while \cd{inRecPath} is already true, the parser state $(R, p)$ has been visited twice on one path, which is precisely the dynamic detection of a left-recursive cycle required by Lemma~\ref{lem:dynamic}, achieved without any separate visited-set data structure.

    \item \cd{foundLeftRec} is set by the descendant frame at the moment a cycle is first detected, that is, when $(R, p)$ is re-entered and no match result exists yet. Since the two frames share the entry, setting this flag \emph{signals to the ancestral frame} that its rule is the head of a left-recursive cycle. The ancestor notices the flag after its recursive call returns, and responds by iteratively re-matching the rule, that is, by expanding the cycle bottom-up per Lemma~\ref{lem:bottomup}.

    \item \cd{memoVersion} records the value of \cd{memoVersionForPos[pos]} at the time this entry was last written or validated. A memo entry whose \cd{memoVersion} is behind the current value is stale: expansion work has happened at this position since the entry was last computed, so its result may be improvable, and the rule must be re-matched rather than served from the memo.
\end{itemize}

The control flow composes these fields as follows. A call finds the entry fresh (non-null \cd{match} and current \cd{memoVersion}), and returns the memoized match: this is the packrat fast path, and it is also taken by every re-entry into the cycle \emph{during} expansion, which is what lets a taller match incorporate the previous, shorter match of the same rule as a subtree, looked up in the memo, rather than recomputing it. Or the call detects re-entry (\cd{inRecPath} true) with no result yet: this frame is the fixed point of Lemma~\ref{lem:fixpoint}, so it seeds the entry with \cd{MISMATCH}, sets \cd{foundLeftRec}, and returns the mismatch. Otherwise the call recurses into the rule's clause tree inside the while-loop. The comparison against the previous best match is made \emph{before} the entry is overwritten, so only a strictly longer match is stored. If \cd{foundLeftRec} was never set, no cycle passes through this entry, the loop exits after one iteration, and the algorithm has behaved exactly as a packrat parser. If \cd{foundLeftRec} was set, the loop continues: each iteration increments \cd{memoVersionForPos[pos]}, invalidating the memo entries at this position so that the next iteration re-matches them against the now-longer seed, and expansion terminates at the first iteration that fails to grow the match, per the stopping criterion of Lemma~\ref{lem:fixpoint}. Note that the loop breaks \emph{after} the longer match has been stored, so the longest match found is what remains in the memo entry. On exit, \cd{inRecPath} is cleared and \cd{memoVersion} is brought current, marking the entry fresh for subsequent callers.

Among the clause types, \cd{RuleRef} is the special one. Every other clause type recurses into its subclauses by direct, unmemoized method calls; only \cd{RuleRef} routes back through \cd{parser.match}, and therefore through the memo table. Memoization consequently happens at the granularity of whole rules rather than of every interior clause. This choice roughly doubles parsing speed in our measurements, because a memo table access costs more than a direct call, but speed turns out not to be the only reason for it.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Why memoization remains valid during expansion}
\label{sec:purity}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Memoization is only sound for pure functions: two identical calls must return identical results. Standard packrat parsing satisfies this by construction, since matching a given rule at a given position always yields the same result, and the memo table is keyed by $(R, p)$ accordingly. Left-recursive expansion appears to break the assumption: each expansion iteration can store a new, longer match for the same $(R, p)$, and a taller match deliberately reads the previous, shorter match of the same rule out of the memo table and incorporates it as a subtree. The recursion is no longer pure, because the memo table has become external state that changes between calls.

The resolution is to observe that the memo table is conceptually keyed not by $(R, p)$ but by $(R, p, v)$, where $v$ is the number of expansion iterations performed at $p$, exactly the quantity that \cd{memoVersionForPos[pos]} counts. Each iteration of the expansion loop increments $v$, and within a single value of $v$ the parser is pure: matching $R$ at $p$ during iteration $v$ always returns the result computed against the seed of iteration $v - 1$. The hidden external state has been made a component of the key, and memoization is sound again. Physically storing all versions is unnecessary, because a caller only ever needs the newest: a memo entry with \cd{memoVersion} equal to the current version is a valid hit, and an entry that is behind is stale and must be recomputed, possibly incorporating the previous match as a subtree. The comparison \cd{memoVersion < parser.memoVersionForPos[pos]} in Fig.~\ref{fig:Code} is exactly this staleness test, and it collapses the conceptual three-component key back to the physical two-component table.

The versioning mechanism serves three distinct purposes. First, it permits re-evaluation at all: without it, the memo entry written by a previous iteration would satisfy every subsequent lookup, and expansion could never make progress. Second, it deduplicates ``cousin'' references. In the rule $A \leftarrow (A \; \texttt{'x'}) \; / \; (A \; \texttt{'y'}) \; / \; B$, both alternatives refer to $A$ at the same position; the first lookup in an iteration re-matches the rule, and the entry's \cd{memoVersion} is then brought current, so the second lookup is a plain memo hit rather than duplicated work. Third, it makes the left recursion machinery free when unused: if no cycle is detected at position $p$, then \cd{memoVersionForPos[p]} is never incremented, every entry at $p$ remains permanently fresh, and the algorithm's behavior and cost are exactly those of standard packrat parsing. The squirrel parsing algorithm is thus a strict superset of the packrat parsing algorithm.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Granularity of memoization}
\label{sec:granularity}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The stopping criterion of Lemma~\ref{lem:fixpoint} forbids ever replacing a match with a mismatch in the memo table. This rule interacts with the \cd{First} operator in a way that makes the \emph{granularity} of memoization semantically significant, not merely a performance choice.

Consider the grammar $A \leftarrow B \; / \; \texttt{'x'}; \;\; B \leftarrow (A \; \texttt{'y'}) \; / \; (A \; \texttt{'x'})$ on the input \cd{xxyx} (this is Fig.~\ref{fig:LeftRecTypes}(b)). The full input is matched by $B$ choosing its second alternative at the top: $(A \; \texttt{'x'})$ with $A$ matching \cd{xxy}. Reaching that parse requires $B$'s \emph{first} alternative $(A \; \texttt{'y'})$, which matched \cd{xxy} in an earlier expansion iteration, to fail in the final iteration, where $A$ has grown to match \cd{xxy} and \cd{'y'} does not follow. If every interior clause were memoized, the entry for the clause $(A \; \texttt{'y'})$ would hold its earlier match of \cd{xxy}, the never-downgrade rule would preserve that match, $B$ could never fail over to its second alternative, and the parse would stop after \cd{xxy}, rejecting the full input. With memoization at rule granularity there is no memo entry for the interior clause $(A \; \texttt{'y'})$: each expansion iteration re-runs $B$'s \cd{First} clause afresh, the first alternative genuinely fails in the final iteration, and the second alternative matches the whole input. The reference implementation, which memoizes at rule granularity, parses this example fully, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:LeftRecTypes}(b).

The never-downgrade rule must therefore apply exactly at the granularity at which expansion is seeded, the whole rule, and not inside the rule's clause tree, where \cd{First} must remain free to re-decide its alternative on every iteration as the seed grows.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Expansion semantics}
\label{sec:semantics}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A useful reference point for the semantics of left-recursive PEG matching is an imagined \emph{omniscient parser}: a nondeterministic parser that considers all possible heights of a left-recursive subtree in parallel, discards those that do not fit the input, and keeps the parse that greedy PEG semantics would prefer. The omniscient parser knows \emph{a priori} exactly how many loops around each cycle will match, and so builds the parse tree top-down; the squirrel parser discovers the same information bottom-up, by monotonic expansion from the fixed point.

For every grammar in Fig.~\ref{fig:LeftRecTypes}, and every grammar in the reference implementation's test suite, the two coincide. They are not, however, identical in general, and the divergence is a direct consequence of the stopping criterion, so we state it precisely rather than leave it as folklore. Consider
\[
A \leftarrow (B \; \texttt{!'y'}) \; / \; \texttt{'x'}; \qquad B \leftarrow A \; \texttt{'x'}
\]
on the input \cd{xxxy}. The omniscient parser reasons as follows: $A$'s first alternative grows through the cycle to match \cd{xx} and then \cd{xxx}, but the full-height match \cd{xxx} is followed by \cd{y}, which the lookahead \cd{!'y'} rejects; each shorter height, \cd{xx} and \cd{x}, is likewise rejected, since it, too, is followed by an \cd{x} that $B$ would have consumed; so the first alternative fails outright, $A$ fails over to its second alternative, and the final match is \cd{x}, of length 1. The squirrel parser instead expands monotonically: the seed mismatch causes the first alternative to fail, \cd{'x'} matches (length 1), and expansion then grows the match to \cd{xx} (length 2), at which point the next iteration's candidate \cd{xxx} is rejected by the lookahead, the match length stops increasing, and expansion stops. The never-downgrade rule retains the length-2 match; the parser does not revisit the failover decision. We verified this behavior in the reference implementation: the parse matches exactly two characters and reports the remaining \cd{xy} as unmatched.

The squirrel parser's semantics for a left-recursive rule is therefore: \emph{the longest match reachable by monotonic bottom-up expansion, with ordered choice re-decided freshly in each iteration, and no backtracking across iterations once expansion stops}. This is a greedy semantics, in the same spirit as PEG's ordered choice itself, which also commits and does not revisit. Bounded left recursion~\cite{medeiros2014} makes the analogous commitment, growing a bound while the match length increases; grammars that require abandoning an already-grown match to accept a shorter one, such as the example above, are pathological for this entire family of semantics. We have not encountered such a grammar outside deliberately constructed examples, all of which involve a lookahead or ordered choice conditioned on input \emph{beyond} the left-recursive match.

We contend the right-associative result is the correct one, by the following analogy to non-left-recursive parsing. In the absence of left recursion, recursive descent follows the leftmost edge of the grammar as its primary axis of motion, then, before exiting each frame, matches the remaining subclauses to the right, collecting the operands after the first into a subtree on the right: this is exactly the traversal order that produces the right-associative tree of panel (h). The first operand of the ambiguous rule (i) binds at the fixed point of the expansion, and the remainder of the input is collected by the second, non-left-recursive occurrence of $E$, whose traversal order is that of (h). Greedy matching constrains the total length of a match, which is the whole input in all three panels; it does not by itself dictate where the interior operator boundaries fall, and the traversal-order argument fills precisely that gap consistently for both the left-recursive and non-left-recursive occurrences of the rule.

\section{Complexity}
\label{sec:complexity}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

We measure parser work in \emph{rule-match computations}: the number of times \cd{MemoEntry.match} computes a result by recursing into a rule's clause tree, rather than returning a memoized result. Each computation costs time proportional to the size of the rule's clause tree, a constant for a fixed grammar, with every embedded rule reference costing $O(1)$ because it either hits the memo table or is accounted as a rule-match computation of its own. We write $|G|$ for the number of rules, and assume, following Ford's analysis of packrat parsing~\cite{ford2002}, that repetition operators are desugared into right recursion (or, equivalently, that repetition bodies are rule references), so that each rule-match computation is $O(1)$; the reference implementation's statistics counter counts exactly these computations.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Reduction to packrat parsing}
\label{sec:packratreduction}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

If no left-recursive cycle is encountered while parsing a given input, then no call to \cd{MemoEntry.match} ever finds \cd{inRecPath} true, so \cd{foundLeftRec} is never set, the while-loop of Fig.~\ref{fig:Code} exits after its first iteration for every entry, and \cd{memoVersionForPos} remains all-zero, so no memo entry is ever stale. Under these conditions every line of \cd{MemoEntry.match} that distinguishes the squirrel parser from a packrat parser is inert, and the two algorithms make identical sequences of calls.

\begin{theorem}[Packrat reduction]
\label{thm:reduction}
On any input for which no left-recursive cycle is encountered, the squirrel parser performs exactly the memoized match computations of a standard packrat parser, at most one per parser state $(R, p)$, and therefore runs in $O(n \cdot |G|)$ time~\cite{ford2002}. The overhead of the left recursion machinery on such inputs is one flag write, one flag clear, one version comparison, and one version copy per memo miss.
\end{theorem}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Cost of left-recursive expansion}
\label{sec:expansioncost}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Call each iteration of the while-loop of Fig.~\ref{fig:Code} beyond the first, for a given memo entry, an \emph{expansion step}, and let $E$ denote the total number of expansion steps over an entire parse. An expansion step at head $(R, p)$ increments \cd{memoVersionForPos[p]}, which invalidates memo entries at position $p$ only: entries at every other position remain fresh, so the re-match performed by the step reuses them, and the step's cost is the cost of freshly re-matching stale rules at position $p$, at most $O(|G|)$ rule-match computations and as few as $O(1)$. An expansion step is taken only when the previous iteration strictly grew the match at its head (the loop otherwise breaks), so the number of steps at head $(R, p)$ is bounded by the final match length at that head, plus two for the seeding and terminating iterations.

\begin{theorem}[General time bound]
\label{thm:general}
A squirrel parse runs in $O(|G| \cdot (n + E))$ time, where $E$ is the total number of expansion steps, and $E \leq \sum_{h} (\ell_h + 2)$ over the left recursion heads $h$ encountered, with final match length $\ell_h \leq n - p_h$ at head position $p_h$.
\end{theorem}

For grammars used in practice, $E$ is linear in $n$. In an expression grammar such as Fig.~\ref{fig:LeftRecTypes}(g), the left-recursive rule has a single head per expression, each expansion step consumes one further operator and operand of that expression, and the spans consumed by distinct heads' expansions are disjoint, so $E$ is at most the number of operators in the input.

\begin{corollary}[Disjoint expansion regions]
\label{cor:linear}
If the input spans consumed by the expansion steps of distinct left recursion heads are pairwise disjoint, then $E = O(n)$ and the parse runs in $O(n \cdot |G|)$ time. This condition holds for every grammar in Fig.~\ref{fig:LeftRecTypes} and for every practical left-recursive grammar we have examined.
\end{corollary}

The condition of Corollary~\ref{cor:linear} is not vacuous, and it would be easy to assume that memoization guarantees linearity unconditionally, so we exhibit the failure case explicitly. The grammar
\[
S \leftarrow (\texttt{\&}E \; \texttt{'x'} \; S) \; / \; \texttt{'x'}; \qquad E \leftarrow E \; \texttt{'x'} \; / \; \texttt{'x'}
\]
matches the input $\texttt{x}^n$, and the lookahead \cd{\&E} forces a full left-recursive expansion of $E$ at \emph{every} input position: $S$ recurses through every position $p$, and the expansion of $E$ at head $(E, p)$ grows from length 1 to length $n - p$, one character per step, before the lookahead succeeds. No memoization can bridge these expansions, because each head grows bottom-up from its own fixed point: the entries $(E, p)$ and $(E, p{+}1)$ are unrelated in the memo table, in contrast to the right-recursive rule $E' \leftarrow \texttt{'x'} \; E' \; / \; \texttt{'x'}$, whose match at $p$ is computed in $O(1)$ from the memoized match at $p + 1$. The total is $E = \sum_{p} (n - p) = \Theta(n^2)$.

Table~\ref{tab:adversarial} confirms the quadratic behavior empirically: doubling the input length multiplies the measured work by $3.96$ to $3.99$, and the work fits $n^2/2$ closely. The same table shows the expression grammar of Fig.~\ref{fig:LeftRecTypes}(g) at exactly one rule-match computation per input character, flat across a 16-fold size range: the practical case of Corollary~\ref{cor:linear}. This quadratic worst case is shared, not introduced: Warth et al.\ note that their seed-growing algorithm is also super-linear in the worst case~\cite{warth2008}, and bounded left recursion re-evaluates a rule once per bound increase in the same pattern~\cite{medeiros2014}. We consider an input-independent characterization of the grammars that can trigger super-linear expansion an open problem.

\subsection{Space}
\label{sec:space}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The memo table holds at most one entry per parser state, $O(n \cdot |G|)$ entries of constant size, and \cd{memoVersionForPos} adds $O(n)$; expansion steps modify entries in place and allocate no new ones. Match objects are shared: a taller match of a left-recursive rule holds a reference to the previous, shorter match as a subtree, so the matches retained by the memo table form a DAG. Under the conditions of Corollary~\ref{cor:linear} the retained matches total $O(n)$ nodes beyond the memo table itself; in the adversarial case above, each head retains a chain of $n - p$ intermediate matches, and space, like time, degrades to quadratic. Recursion depth is bounded by the length of the input plus the depth of the grammar, as for any recursive descent parser.

\section{Why error recovery has resisted recursive descent parsing}
\label{sec:whyhard}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Syntax error recovery has a long literature for table-driven LL and LR parsers, and a remarkably thin one for recursive descent. The asymmetry is structural, not accidental, and it is worth stating its causes precisely, because they jointly motivate the design in Section~\ref{sec:repair}.

A table-driven parser that encounters an error possesses a well-defined machine configuration: a stack of states, a current token, and tables from which the set of acceptable continuations can be read off. Fifty years of recovery methods are built on interrogating that configuration. Burke and Fisher consider single-token insertions, deletions, substitutions, and merges at the error point, validated by checking that the parse can proceed some fixed number of tokens past the repair~\cite{burke1987}; scope and phrase-level recovery extend the same idea. CPCT+ searches the LR automaton's configuration graph breadth-first at the error state for the complete set of minimum-cost repair sequences over token insertions and deletions~\cite{diekmann2020}. De Jonge et al.\ derive \emph{permissive grammars}, with water rules that skip tokens and insertion rules that supply missing ones, automatically from a grammar, for scannerless generalized-LR parsing~\cite{dejonge2012}. Each method reads the parser's frozen state at the failure point and repairs forward from it.

A recursive descent parser at the moment of ultimate failure has no such configuration to interrogate, for four reasons that compound one another.

First, in a backtracking parser, \emph{failure is control flow}. A PEG parser fails constantly and routinely: every \cd{First} alternative that does not match, every \cd{NotFollowedBy} that succeeds, every speculative descent that unwinds is a ``failure,'' indistinguishable in the machinery from the failure that a human would call the syntax error. By the time the top rule definitively rejects the input, the recursion has fully unwound and the interesting configurations are gone. The standard mitigation, reporting the farthest position reached by any match attempt, was already present in Ford's packrat work~\cite{ford2002b}, and identifies a plausible error \emph{location}, but the set of clauses that failed there mixes genuine expectations with exploratory ones, and provides no repair.

Second, \emph{ordered choice commits}. Once \cd{First} selects an alternative, the other alternatives are never revisited, and a rule that has matched is never re-examined (Section~\ref{sec:semantics} extends the same commitment to left-recursive expansion). An error whose natural repair lies inside an already-committed region cannot be reached by any recovery scheme that only acts at the failure frontier: in \cd{\{"a":1,"bc:[2,3]\}} the missing close quote causes a JSON parser to commit to a long string literal \cd{"bc:[2,3]\}} and fail far away, at the end of the input, while the minimal repair inserts one character well \emph{before} the failure point. Configuration-based methods, including CPCT+, repair at or after the point of failure by construction; repairs behind the frontier are structurally out of reach.

Third, \emph{scannerless parsing removes the token safety net}. Classic panic-mode recovery skips to a synchronizing token; PEG parsers of the packrat family parse at character granularity, where no reserved delimiter set exists and any ``synchronizing'' character may equally occur inside a string literal or comment. Recovery mechanisms that have been proposed for PEGs re-introduce synchronization by hand: labeled failures with per-label recovery expressions require the grammar author to anticipate error points and write recovery expressions for them~\cite{medeiros2018}, and the follow-up work inserts such expressions automatically using FIRST- and FOLLOW-set heuristics~\cite{medeiros2020}. These methods make PEG recovery practical, at the cost of per-grammar engineering or heuristic annotation, and the recovered parse has no characterization beyond the behavior of the annotations themselves.

Fourth, and specific to the setting of this paper, \emph{recovery machinery woven into a memoizing parser corrupts the memo table's meaning}. A memo entry is sound because matching a rule at a position always yields the same result (Section~\ref{sec:purity}); a recovery mode that skips characters or fabricates matches makes results depend on recovery context, which is not part of the memo key. With left recursion the interaction is worse, since expansion relies on the never-downgrade rule and version invalidation, and recovery-induced matches feed back into seeds. We report a cautionary datum: an earlier design of ours that threaded error tolerance through the squirrel matching algorithm itself, propagating per-clause skip bounds through the memo table, produced cascading errors on 79\% of the single-character mutations of a valid JSON document, recovering the ideal single-error parse for only 2\%, under exhaustive single-character mutation of the same 48-character document used in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. The failure mode was not a bug but the architecture: each locally tolerated error perturbed downstream memoization and choice commitment, manufacturing further spurious errors.

The pika parser~\cite{hutchison2020} escapes several of these constraints by abandoning recursive descent altogether, parsing bottom-up and right to left by dynamic programming, so that structure after an error is recovered independently of the error; but it pays a large constant-factor cost on every input, valid or not, and its recovery is organized around designated rules of interest rather than a criterion of repair quality.

What is common to every approach above is that it modifies the parser, or the grammar, to tolerate errors. The parser is the most delicate machine in the system, and its invariants, greedy choice, memoization purity, and monotonic left-recursive expansion, are exactly what error tolerance perturbs.

\section{Error recovery as a costed second descent}
\label{sec:repair}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

We leave the parser of Sections~\ref{sec:algorithm}--\ref{sec:complexity} untouched and add recovery beside it, as a second descent over the same grammar and the same input, in which a clause that cannot match becomes a priced alternative rather than a dead end. The natural objective for any such search is nearness: repair the invalid input to the closest member of the language. Following Damerau's observation that over 80 percent of human transcription errors are a single insertion, deletion, substitution, or transposition~\cite{damerau1964,levenshtein1966}, nearness is counted in single-character edit operations, and the distance from an input $s$ to the language is
\begin{equation}
d(s, L(G)) \;=\; \min_{t \in L(G)} d_{\mathrm{DL}}(s, t),
\label{eq:dist}
\end{equation}
where $d_{\mathrm{DL}}$ is the Damerau-Levenshtein distance between two strings.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Hardness of exact minimum-distance repair}
\label{sec:hardness}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Exact minimization of Eq.~\ref{eq:dist} is intractable for PEGs in general, and the obstruction is syntactic lookahead: a conjunction of lookaheads constrains characters that a repair must \emph{invent}, and choosing inventions that satisfy all of them simultaneously encodes satisfiability.

Define the decision problem \textsc{Min-PEG-Repair}: given a PEG $G$, a string $s$, and an integer $d$, decide whether $d(s, L(G)) \le d$.

\begin{theorem}[Hardness of exact repair]
\label{thm:hardness}
\textsc{Min-PEG-Repair} is NP-complete, and remains NP-hard for the fixed input $s = \cd{@}$, with the grammar and $d$ alone encoding the instance.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Membership in NP: a witness is $s' \in L(G)$ at distance at most $d$; its length is at most $|s| + d$, and PEG membership is decidable in polynomial time by packrat parsing.

Hardness, by reduction from 3-SAT. Let $\varphi$ have variables $x_1, \ldots, x_m$ and clauses $C_1, \ldots, C_r$. Writing $\cd{.}^{k}$ for a sequence of $k$ occurrences of the any-character terminal (PEG has no counting operator; the expansion is linear in $k$), construct:
\begin{align*}
\mathit{Top} &\leftarrow \cd{@} \; (\mathit{Sat} \; / \; \mathit{Fb})\\
\mathit{Sat} &\leftarrow \mathop{\&}\mathit{K}_1 \; \mathop{\&}\mathit{K}_2 \cdots \mathop{\&}\mathit{K}_r \;\; \mathit{Bit}^{\,m} \; \mathop{!}\cd{.}
\qquad \mathit{Bit} \leftarrow \cd{0} / \cd{1}\\
\mathit{K}_j &\leftarrow L_{j,1} \; / \; L_{j,2} \; / \; L_{j,3}
\qquad\quad L_{j,k} \leftarrow \cd{.}^{\,i-1}\,\cd{1} \;\;\text{for literal } x_i,\;\; \cd{.}^{\,i-1}\,\cd{0} \;\;\text{for } \lnot x_i\\
\mathit{Fb} &\leftarrow \cd{\#}^{\,m+1} \; \mathop{!}\cd{.}
\end{align*}
The grammar has size $O(rm)$. On the repaired string $\cd{@}\,w$, each lookahead $\mathop{\&}\mathit{K}_j$ inspects the window $w$ and succeeds precisely when clause $C_j$ is satisfied by the assignment that $w$ spells; $\mathit{Bit}^{\,m}\,\mathop{!}\cd{.}$ forces $w$ to be exactly $m$ bits. So $L(\mathit{Top}) = \{\cd{@}w : w \in \{0,1\}^m \text{ satisfies } \varphi\} \cup \{\cd{@}\cd{\#}^{m+1}\}$. Every member begins with \cd{@} and has length at least $m + 1$, so from the input $s = \cd{@}$ the minimum distance is exactly $m$ (insert a satisfying assignment) when $\varphi$ is satisfiable, and exactly $m + 1$ (insert the fallback) otherwise. Taking $d = m$ decides $\varphi$.
\end{proof}

Three scoping remarks make the theorem useful rather than merely discouraging. First, the hardness is in \emph{combined} complexity: the grammar carries the formula. For a fixed grammar, the joint state of all pending lookahead decisions ranges over a set whose size depends on $G$ alone, so repair is polynomial in $n$ and $d$ with a grammar-dependent constant; the theorem says only that this constant cannot be uniformly polynomial in $|G|$ unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$. Second, the difficulty lives entirely in \emph{invented} text: the reduction's instance is the one-character input \cd{@}, and everything the lookaheads jointly constrain is the window $w$ that the repair must conjure out of nothing. A recovery that reports absent text as absent, instead of spelling it, never chooses the contents of $w$, and so never decides the satisfiability question; the intractability attaches to the invention, and only the invention. Third, the objective itself is untouched: the theorem bounds the cost of \emph{finding} the minimum, not the coherence of defining it.

One might hope to sidestep the theorem with a tabulating parser. The pika parser~\cite{hutchison2020}, for instance, parses bottom-up and right to left in linear time for a fixed grammar, and its memo table ends up holding every match of every clause at every position of the input, from which a repair can be assembled by stitching matches across damaged regions. The hope fails on exactly the hard instances, and seeing why is instructive. Any table built over the \emph{given} input, whether top-down or bottom-up, contains facts about the characters that are present; the reduction places the entire difficulty in characters that are \emph{absent} --- the invented window $w$ --- constrained jointly by the conjunction of lookaheads. A per-clause cheapest-completion computation prices each lookahead independently and yields $m$, a lower bound; deciding whether the joint constraints admit that bound \emph{is} the satisfiability question, and no polynomial-size table over the one-character input can carry the answer. Tabulation changes where parsing effort is spent; it does not change what repair must decide.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{A frontier lower bound for compositional repair}
\label{sec:frontier}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Theorem~\ref{thm:hardness} bounds repair in the lookahead dimension: joint constraints on invented characters make the \emph{time} of exact repair super-polynomial in the grammar. A second, independent obstruction lives in what we call the frontier dimension, and it bounds \emph{state}: it explains why every efficient repairer --- the one in this paper included --- carries set-valued intermediate results, and why no dramatically smaller memoized implementation can exist.

For a clause $C$ and input position $i$, call the \emph{frontier} of $C$ at $i$ the set of pairs $(e, \omega)$ --- an end position and a pending single-character obligation --- reachable by some repair of the text $C$ consumes, each carrying its minimal cost. A \emph{compositional} evaluator is one that visits $C$ at $i$ once, retains a summary, and afterwards answers every continuation's question --- what completing the parse from each endpoint costs --- from the summary alone, without re-entering $C$, re-reading its definition, or posing membership queries about strings other than the input. A memo table entry is precisely such a summary.

\begin{theorem}[Frontier lower bound]
\label{thm:frontier}
For every $m$ there are grammars of size $O(m^2)$ and inputs of length $2m$ on which any exact compositional repairer's summary of one clause at one position requires $\Omega(m)$ bits --- as many bits as the frontier holds entries.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A one-way communication argument in the style of \textsc{Index}. For $S \subseteq \{1, \ldots, m\}$ define
\begin{align*}
P_p &\leftarrow \cd{x}^{2p-1}\,\cd{a} \quad (p \in S), \qquad\qquad P_p \leftarrow \cd{x}^{2p-1}\,\cd{a}\,\cd{b} \quad (p \notin S),\\
C_S &\leftarrow P_1 \,/\, P_2 \,/\, \cdots \,/\, P_m,
\end{align*}
and take the input $s = \cd{x}^{2m}$. Substituting \cd{a} at position $2p-1$ repairs $P_p$ at cost one, ending at the even cursor $2p$, precisely when $p \in S$; insertion-based repairs end at odd cursors, so the even cost-one frontier of $C_S$ at position $0$ spells out $S$. On the \emph{given} input every branch fails at position $2p-1$ before its trailing \cd{b} is ever consulted, so the parse behavior a compositional evaluator observes is identical for all $2^m$ subsets. Now the continuation $K_t \leftarrow \cd{x}^{2m-2t}\,\mathop{!}\cd{.}$ makes $d(s,\, L(C_S\, K_t)) \le 1$ hold exactly when $t \in S$: the cost-one member $\cd{x}^{2t-1}\cd{a}\,\cd{x}^{2m-2t}$ exists only for $t \in S$, and every other member differs from $s$ in length or in two characters. Two subsets $S \ne S'$ force different answers for some continuation $K_t$ with $t$ in their symmetric difference, so all $2^m$ subsets need distinct summaries: $\Omega(m)$ bits.
\end{proof}

The theorem is deliberately scoped to summaries, and its escape hatches are instructive, because each is a known architecture. An algorithm may \emph{recompute} instead of summarize --- enumerate candidate strings in cost order and re-parse each with the unmodified parser; that is exact even under full PEG semantics with constant repair state, at exponential time. It may \emph{specialize}: index repair facts by their continuation, multiplying rule identities rather than enriching values. Or it may \emph{suspend}: retain the parser's continuation itself and resume it as invented characters are bound. Materialized frontiers, specialized facts, suspended continuations, or recomputation --- the information the theorem counts must live in one of these places, and the choice is an engineering trade, not an escape. The two-symbol adversarial family above realizes frontiers that grow linearly in the input at a single cell.

The classical error-correcting parsers of Aho and Peterson~\cite{aho1972} and Lyon~\cite{lyon1974} compute minimum-distance CFG repair by weighted parsing of a covering grammar --- repair as recognition under a grammar--transducer product --- and the same object has recently been restated for CFGs as language intersection with an edit-distance automaton~\cite{considine2025}. The PEG setting adds the two dimensions bounded in this section: lookahead makes joint constraints on invented text intractable (Theorem~\ref{thm:hardness}), and the frontier is the irreducible currency of any efficient evaluation (Theorem~\ref{thm:frontier}).

\subsection{The recovered parse}
\label{sec:contract}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Theorems~\ref{thm:hardness} and~\ref{thm:frontier} leave a definite corridor. The intractability of Eq.~\ref{eq:dist} attaches to invented text alone, and any efficient compositional search must carry a set of priced endpoints per clause and position. The implementation therefore changes what recovery returns: a parse of the input as it stands, in which damage is marked rather than mended.

\begin{definition}[Recovered parse]
\label{def:recovered}
Given a grammar $G$ and an input $s \notin L(G)$, a \emph{recovered parse} of $s$ is a complete parse tree over $s$ in which every character of $s$ is either consumed by an ordinary match or covered by a \emph{deletion mark}, a syntax-error node spanning characters read as noise, and in which a zero-width \emph{missing mark} stands wherever a clause required something the input does not supply. The \emph{cost} of a recovered parse is the number of characters under deletion marks, plus the number of missing marks before the end of the input, plus one if anything is missing at the end of the input.
\end{definition}

No character is ever invented. A missing mark records that, say, a digit was required, without choosing which digit; the choice is exactly what Theorem~\ref{thm:hardness} makes intractable, and a chosen digit would in any case show the user a guess. Deletion marks and missing marks correspond to the deletion and insertion operations of $d_{\mathrm{DL}}$ with insertions reported rather than performed. Substitution is offered exactly where it chooses nothing: where the grammar spells out the required text, one wrong character may be consumed as a deletion mark standing in that text's place, a single edit whose meaning the grammar itself supplies (Section~\ref{sec:slots}). A character class names no single text a wrong character could stand for, so there the wrong character costs a deletion plus a missing mark, and transpositions are not offered. The end of the input is charged once, however many required pieces the cut-off strands, because a truncated document witnesses one fact, that the text stops, and the number of obligations dangling at the cut measures the grammar's shape more than the input's damage. The engine returns a recovered parse of minimal cost under this model.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Readings and their judgment}
\label{sec:readings}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The unit the costed descent manipulates is the priced counterpart of a match. A \emph{reading} of a clause at a position is one way that clause can account for a stretch of input starting there: an end position, the piece of parse tree it contributes, and a bill of five counters: \cd{del}, input characters deleted as noise; \cd{miss}, missing marks before the end of the input; \cd{missEnd}, missing marks at the end of the input; \cd{evid}, \emph{evidence}, the count of characters matched by picky terminals, literals, single characters, and non-inverted character classes, which could only have matched because the input really contains those characters (a wildcard or inverted class accepts almost anything, so its matches prove nothing; nor do characters matched by a terminal that is the whole body of a repetition permitted to match nothing, whitespace in most grammars, since text every reading may swallow in any number distinguishes none of them); and \cd{pen}, penalty points, extra charges that make a reading lose comparisons without ever appearing in the tree or the reported cost. A reading also records \cd{doubt} and \cd{lastDoubt}, the positions of its first and its latest repair; between them they bracket where its trouble lies. A reading's cost is $\cd{del} + \cd{miss} + [\cd{missEnd} > 0]$, per Definition~\ref{def:recovered}, and its \emph{absorbed} count, judged from a position $q$, is its span from $q$ minus \cd{del} minus \cd{evid}: the characters it consumed without proving anything. A reading with an empty bill is \emph{clean}; a clean reading that also makes exactly the plain parser's greedy choices is \emph{preferred}. Readings are stored as backward-linked chains of steps with these sums carried alongside, so comparison is constant-time and only the eventual winner's chain is materialized into a tree.

Reading $a$ beats reading $b$, judged from position $q$, by the first of seven comparisons that differs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Lower total charge: cost, plus \cd{pen}, plus an \emph{absorb penalty} of one against a repaired reading whose absorbed count from $q$ exceeds its evidence. A reading that mostly swallowed the input has not explained it. The absorb penalty is recomputed from the reading's own sums at every comparison and never stored, so it cannot compound however many nesting levels judge the same span.
    \item A preferred reading beats any rival: where the input parses as it stands, the plain parser's reading is the meaning of the document.
    \item More evidence.
    \item Later first doubt: \cd{doubt} combines as a minimum under chain extension, and the reading that stayed faithful to the input longer wins.
    \item Earlier last doubt: \cd{lastDoubt} combines as a maximum, and among readings whose faith first broke at the same place, the one whose trouble is confined earliest wins, because edits clustered at the flaw beat an account that spreads a second edit into text the input got right.
    \item Fewer missing marks at the end of the input. The cost collapsed those to a single charge; the cardinality it forgot decides here, so a completion stranding two constructs beats one stranding five.
    \item Fewer implied mismatches: the smaller of \cd{del} and $\cd{miss} + \cd{missEnd}$ is the number of deletion--absence pairs the account can be read as, each a tacit claim that the input held one thing where the grammar required another; edits of one kind are the plainer story.
\end{enumerate}
A tie after all seven keys goes to the \emph{newest} candidate, which is deterministic because candidate generation order is; this comparison completes Definition~\ref{def:recovered}, and recovery is thereby a function of grammar and input.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The costed descent}
\label{sec:descent}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The descent mirrors Section~\ref{sec:algorithm} structurally. For every composite clause and input position there is a \emph{repair cell} holding the best reading for each end position reached, exactly the frontier of Theorem~\ref{thm:frontier} with the theorem's $(e, \omega)$ pairs realized as (end, reading); offering a reading to a cell is also the improvement test, since the offer reports whether it displaced the holder of its ending, and a fixed point is reached when a whole proposal pass displaces nothing.

The search runs in rounds with a repair budget $b = 1, 2, \ldots$, and within a round a reading may accumulate $\cd{del} + \cd{miss} \le b$. The end-of-input counter is excluded from the budget: the budget is split additively down a sequence (each subclause is handed what the partial reading before it has not spent), and the end-of-input charge is idempotent, paid once for the whole parse, so admitting it into the split would re-charge it at every nesting level that touches the end of the input. It is charged globally instead, at the root, and correspondingly a terminal's missing reading is offered at the end of the input even when the budget is spent. No repair can cost more than deleting all $n$ characters and marking the shortest document the grammar accepts as missing, and that shortest length is computable from the grammar, so the ladder is bounded and recovery always terminates. The first round that produces a winner ends the search, which is what makes lightly damaged input cheap: a one-edit error is found at budget 1, whatever the worst case costs.

A budget of zero is the plain parser. A reading that has spent everything can only continue by reading the input exactly as it stands, so a clause queried with no budget is answered directly by the plain parser of Section~\ref{sec:algorithm}, packaged once as a preferred reading and cached beside the memo entry that produced it. This is the single doorway between the two descents: the plain parser is consulted, never modified, which is how recovery coexists with the invariants that Section~\ref{sec:whyhard} showed error tolerance perturbs.

Left recursion needs nothing new. A repair cell carries the same three fields as the parser's memo entry, and the same protocol runs over readings: re-entry into a cell being computed seeds the cycle, the seed is served raw, unfiltered by budget, because the growth must build on the seed's repair-carrying readings; the owning frame re-proposes until a pass improves nothing; and each growth pass bumps a per-position version, so cells computed against the half-grown seed answer again when next consulted. One refinement earns its keep: each cell records whether its computation ever read a growing seed, and only such cells consult the version, since a cell that read no seed cannot be stale, whatever grew; everything else it read is indexed by position, and growth changes only the growing cell.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Sequences: where repairs enter}
\label{sec:slots}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A sequence folds its subclauses left to right over a set of partial readings, and every repair enters the search here, through one of three moves tried for each partial reading $r$ and next subclause $C$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Use} $C$'s own readings at $r$'s end, computed under the budget $r$ has not spent. A terminal that matches contributes its match as a reading, with evidence equal to its length if it is picky; a terminal that cannot match contributes the zero-width reading that records it missing. Obligations therefore flow through sequencing with no separate machinery: a sequence whose third element is absent simply carries a missing mark where the third element would have begun.
    \item \emph{Delete ahead}: where $C$ has no clean reading at $r$'s end, skip characters, one deletion each and only as far as the remaining budget allows, up to the \emph{first} position at which the plain parser reads $C$; the finished parse found there is reused whole, so the skipped span is the entire price of the move.
    \item \emph{Substitute}: where $C$ is an exact-text terminal, a literal or a single character, one wrong character may be consumed as a deletion mark standing in $C$'s place, the single-edit substitution of Section~\ref{sec:contract}. Two gates keep the move from degenerating into a guess. It is offered only when the partial reading before it is the plain parser's own, so substitutions never chain off already-repaired text; and only when the sequence \emph{resumes} just past the swap: the remaining subclauses are read with the plain parser from the next position, the first that consumes input confirms the move, a failure vetoes it, and a sequence with nothing left to read is confirmation as well. A swap the rest of the sequence cannot read from is not a substitution but a guess, and each one opens a position the search would never otherwise visit.
\end{enumerate}
A multi-character literal that fails to match whole is itself read as a sequence of its characters by this same fold, yielding partial prefixes, interior deletions, and single-character substitutions with no alignment code: \cd{i"} reads as the keyword \cd{if} at cost one.

One rule adjudicates between moves 1 and 2 when both apply. If deleting $k$ characters ahead reached a real parse of $C$, then a rival option that stands still and declares $C$ missing, offering no evidence and carrying at least $k$ missing marks, takes one penalty point: at equal price, text read as noise beats constructs declared absent, because the noise is on the page and the absent construct is a guess. Three exemptions are load-bearing: two concern what is being priced, and one concerns who may price it. A subclause with only one possible tree shape is exempt, because its absence is unambiguous rather than a guess; the left-recursive seed is exempt, because the seed's missing reading is the anchor each taller spine of the growth is built on, and penalizing it suppresses the growth itself; and the sequence's \emph{last} subclause is exempt, because what stood at the end of a sequence is a question the enclosing context owns, the parent's next subclause may want exactly the character the deletion would consume, and this fold cannot see that far. The rule therefore fires only where a deletion was demonstrated nearby, the absence is genuinely ambiguous, and the question is this fold's to answer. The last-subclause exemption is a judgment about jurisdiction rather than a uniform improvement: over the battery of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} it corrects ten cases and costs four.

After each subclause, only the best reading per end position survives, judged by the comparison of Section~\ref{sec:readings}. The fold therefore carries at most one reading per ending at every step, which is what keeps each round polynomial.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The remaining operators}
\label{sec:operators}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

An ordered choice, matched plainly, takes its first matching alternative. Under repair every alternative contributes candidates, and one rule preserves PEG's commitment: once some alternative has read the input exactly as it stands, the choice is \emph{settled}, and a reading from a later alternative is admitted only if its evidence exceeds its absorbed count, that is, only if it proves more than it swallows, and even then without preferred status. The rule exists because a later alternative able to absorb arbitrary text, a quoted-string rule being the standard case, can otherwise buy the entire remaining document for the price of its two delimiters, re-reading a healthy committed prefix as the interior of a string; the settled test is one notch stricter than the absorb penalty (majority rather than half), because here the challenger is trying to overturn a reading the input already supports.

A repetition's readings are found by a reachability sweep: from the empty reading, extend repeatedly by one occurrence of the body, keeping the best reading per end position, until nothing improves, refusing any step that proves nothing, misses nothing, and consumes only deleted characters, because a repetition never requires another occurrence and so may not manufacture one out of noise. Every stopping point is a clean reading with only the farthest preferred, so a continuation elsewhere in the grammar may stop a repetition early at no cost, and a one-or-more repetition that matched nothing owes exactly one occurrence, supplied by the body's own missing readings. An optional clause contributes its child's readings and the empty reading, the empty one keeping preferred status only where the child cannot match plainly. A lookahead consumes nothing and admits no repairs inside itself, since input accepted inside an assertion would be input the parse then refuses to consume; it asks only whether a clean reading of its body exists. A rule reference wraps the rule's name around its body's readings and refuses one kind: a repaired reading that consumed input without proving a character of it, which asserts the rule's presence on no evidence at all. A reading that consumed nothing passes, being an honest missing marker, and so does any reading of a rule with only one possible tree shape, whose presence, as in the penalty rule's one-shape exemption, is no guess.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Rounds, the root, and the winner's tree}
\label{sec:root}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

At each budget, the top rule's readings at position 0 are judged from position 0. A reading that stops short of the end is first extended by a deletion mark spanning the unread tail, the same move as any other deletion, so no separate rule prices trailing garbage. A repair-free reading that is not the plain parser's own is discarded at the root: the plain parser has already rejected the input, so such a reading, reachable only through choices the parser would not make, is an artifact of the search, and returning it would price a rejected document at zero. A reading that ends \emph{incomplete}, with a required piece missing exactly at its end and input still ahead of it, is incoherent as an account of the document, and is held aside: it may displace the round's winner only if it costs no more and proves strictly more, which is what lets an unfinished genuine construct followed by noise beat a dubious construct that swallows everything; and if no budget yields a coherent winner, the best incomplete reading is returned rather than nothing. The first round with a winner ends the search.

The winner's chain is then folded into the output tree, with one rule about names: a construct that matched nothing at the very end of the input loses its name, because the input never reached it and naming it would assert a guess, while a zero-width construct mid-document keeps its name, the surrounding matches having proved which construct it must be. The tree's error content equals the winning reading's bill by construction, one syntax-error node per deleted span and per missing mark, so the reported cost and the returned tree cannot disagree.

    int missEnd, int evid, int doubt,
    int lastDoubt, int pen,
    boolean incomplete,
    Object piece, Reading prev) {
  // piece: SyntaxError, Match, or Labeled
  // lastDoubt: -1 if clean, else the
  // site of the latest repair
  int cost()  { return del + miss
                  + (missEnd > 0 ? 1 : 0); }
  int spent() { return del + miss; }
  boolean clean()     { return doubt >= CLEAN; }
  boolean preferred() { return doubt > CLEAN; }
  int absorbed(int q) {
    return (end - q) - del - evid; }
  int absorbPen(int q) {
    return !clean() && absorbed(q) > evid
        ? 1 : 0; }
  Reading then(Reading s) {
    return new Reading(s.end, del + s.del,
        miss + s.miss, missEnd + s.missEnd,
        evid + s.evid, min(doubt, s.doubt),
        max(lastDoubt, s.lastDoubt),
        pen + s.pen, s.incomplete
            || (s.end == end && incomplete),
        s.piece, this); }
}

int compare(Reading a, Reading b, int q) {
  var ca = a.cost() + a.pen + a.absorbPen(q);
  var cb = b.cost() + b.pen + b.absorbPen(q);
  if (ca != cb) return ca - cb;
  if (a.preferred() != b.preferred())
    return a.preferred() ? -1 : 1;
  if (a.evid != b.evid) return b.evid - a.evid;

  if (a.lastDoubt != b.lastDoubt)
    return a.lastDoubt - b.lastDoubt;
  if (a.missEnd != b.missEnd)
    return a.missEnd - b.missEnd;
  return min(a.del, a.miss + a.missEnd)
      - min(b.del, b.miss + b.missEnd);

  boolean plainKnown; Reading plain;

  boolean add(Reading r, int pos) {
    var b = bestByEnd[r.end];
    if (b != null
        && compare(r, b, pos) > 0)
      return false;
    bestByEnd[r.end] = r;  // a tie: newest
    return b == null
        || compare(r, b, pos) < 0;
  }
  // readings(budget): entries with
  // spent() <= budget; every preferred
  // entry except the farthest-ending
  // is downgraded to merely clean
}


Reading[] grow(Clause c, int pos) {
  var cell = c.cellFor(pos);
  if (cell.inRecPath) {   // seed: serve raw
    cell.foundLeftRec = true;
    seedWasRead = true;
    return cell.readings(UNLIMITED);
  }
  if (budget == 0 && pos < input.length) {
    if (!cell.plainKnown) {
      cell.plainKnown = true;
      cell.plain = c.cleanReading(pos);
    }                     // repair IS parsing
    return cell.plain == null
        ? [] : [cell.plain];
  }
  if (cell.atBudget >= budget
      && (!cell.usedSeed || cell.memoVersion
          == repairVersionForPos[pos]))
    return cell.readings(budget);
  cell.inRecPath = true;
  var outer = seedWasRead;
  seedWasRead = false;
  while (true) {
    var improved = false;
    for (var r : c.propose(pos))
      improved |= cell.add(r, pos);
    if (!improved || !cell.foundLeftRec)
      break;
    cell.memoVersion =
        ++repairVersionForPos[pos];
  }
  cell.usedSeed = seedWasRead;
  seedWasRead = outer || seedWasRead;
  cell.inRecPath = false;
  cell.atBudget = budget;
  cell.memoVersion = repairVersionForPos[pos];
  return cell.readings(budget);
}

Reading[] readSlots(Clause[] slots,
    Clause label, int pos) {
  var cur = [Reading.empty(pos)];
  for (var s = 0; s < slots.length; s++) {
    var slot = slots[s];
    var next = [];
    for (var r : cur) {
      if (r.spent() > budget) continue;
      var whole = budget;
      budget = whole - r.spent();
      var opts = slot.readings(r.end);
      budget = whole;
      var clean = opts.any(o -> o.clean());
      var k = -1;
      if (!clean) {
        for (var j = r.end + 1; // delete ahead
             j <= r.end + budget - r.cost()
                 && j <= input.length; j++) {
          var p = slot.cleanReading(j);
          if (p == null) continue;
          next.add(r.then(Reading.deleting(
              r.end, j)).then(p));
          k = j - r.end;
          break;
        }
        if (r.preferred()       // substitute
            && r.spent() < budget
            && r.end < input.length
            && slot.isExactText()
            && resumes(slots, s, r.end + 1))
          next.add(r.then(Reading.deleting(
              r.end, r.end + 1)));
      }
      var pen = k > 0
          && s < slots.length - 1
          && !slot.isGrowingSeedAt(r.end)
          && !slot.hasOneShape();
      for (var o : opts)        // use
        next.add(r.then(
            pen && o.end == r.end
                && !o.clean() && k <= o.miss
            ? o.penalized() : o));
    }
    if (next.isEmpty()) return [];
    cur = bestPerEnd(next, pos);
  }
  return [for (var r : bestPerEnd(cur, pos))
      r.labeled(label, pos)];
}

boolean resumes(Clause[] slots,
    int s, int at) {
  for (var i = s + 1; i < slots.length; i++) {

\section{Evaluation of error recovery}
\label{sec:evaluation}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

All measurements in this section use the Dart reference implementation (Section~\ref{sec:software}), single-threaded on an AMD Ryzen 9 3950X under Dart 3.12.2.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Methodology: an exhaustive mutation battery with frozen expectations}
\label{sec:methodology}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Recovery quality is easy to overstate with anecdotal examples, so the evaluation is exhaustive within its scope and scored against expectations fixed before recovery runs. Three grammars are used: the complete JSON grammar of Fig.~\ref{fig:JsonGrammar}; a statement language with blocks, conditionals, assignments, string literals, and a negative-lookahead keyword guard; and the left-recursive expression grammar of Fig.~\ref{fig:BatteryGrammars}, so that every reading the recovery produces over an expression flows through the left-recursion machinery of Sections~\ref{sec:algorithm} and~\ref{sec:descent}. Over 23 valid documents chosen to exercise every construct of the three grammars, mutants are generated by the operations a person performs on a document: deleting one character, inserting one character, substituting one character, truncating the document, and a miscellaneous class of transpositions and multi-site damage. Every generated mutant that still parses is discarded, since it needs no recovery. Two mutation families are excluded because no recovery could be scored on them fairly: mutations of a binary operator, where both restorations are one edit away and the original is unrecoverable in principle, and truncation stubs of one or two characters, which carry no decidable evidence of the document they came from. The battery holds 2{,}101 mutants: 525 deletions, 525 truncations, 438 insertions, 350 substitutions, and 263 miscellaneous, spanning all three grammars (944 JSON, 856 statement, 301 expression).

The expected result for each mutant is derived from the \emph{frozen} parser, with no reference to the recovery engine: the plain parse tree of the undamaged original, projected to a skeleton over the grammar's named rules. For truncations, the expectation drops constructs whose evidence lies wholly beyond the cut, since no reader of the truncated text could know of them. A recovered parse is scored as the normalized edit similarity between its skeleton and the expected skeleton, 1.0 meaning the recovery restored exactly the tree a reader of the undamaged document would draw; because the expectations come from the plain parser and the scoring is structural, the engine cannot be tuned toward the metric except by recovering well.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:results}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:battery} summarizes the sweep. Over all 2{,}101 mutants the mean skeleton similarity is 0.990, and 85.8\% of recoveries restore the expected skeleton \emph{exactly}. Truncations, which require completing arbitrarily deep construct spines and which defeat failure-point repair by construction, tie with substitutions, which the engine reads as single edits (Section~\ref{sec:slots}), for the highest class score (0.997). The whole sweep costs about 0.6 seconds of engine time, roughly a third of a millisecond per damaged document. Valid inputs pay nothing beyond the plain parse, which is the search's own zeroth round. The frontier sets of Theorem~\ref{thm:frontier} stay small in practice: over the 622{,}025 repair-cell computations of the sweep, a cell holds a mean of 1.71 readings when its computation completes, with a maximum of 52.

The imperfect remainder is typified by damage the input cannot decide: after \cd{if (a) \{ b=1; \}} degrades to a bare \cd{i}, nothing distinguishes a truncated \cd{if} statement from an assignment to a variable whose name starts with \cd{i}, both readings cost the same, and the deterministic tie rule sometimes picks the reading the frozen expectation did not. No mutant crashes the engine, and none is left without a complete tree.

\subsection{Hard checks the battery cannot express}
\label{sec:hardchecks}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

An aggregate over mutants cannot enforce a hard requirement, because one violated case moves the mean by half a part per thousand, and it cannot see behavior classes that its own construction excludes. Four independent checks therefore run alongside the battery, and the engine passes all of them. First, the acceptance readings: \cd{,3true} inside an array must be read as \cd{,3,true}, a missing separator between two real values, and never by deleting the digit; \cd{[,2,} must be read by deleting the stray comma, the penalty rule's case, and never by declaring some unknowable value missing in front of it; and in a two-rule grammar whose first element is an undetermined character class, recovery must not delete real matching input to make room for a missing mark. Second, a free-span check: on grammars where a loose alternative covers a span at no cost, recovery must not delete characters from input that already matched to reach a better-\emph{explained} reading, a failure mode the battery cannot contain because every battery case genuinely needs repair; the check demands exact repair costs on five probes. Third, a commitment check of sixteen probes: the construct that a healthy prefix has committed to must still head the recovered tree, including on inputs engineered so that a catch-all alternative could swallow the whole document for the price of one invented delimiter, the settled-choice rule's case. Fourth, cost conformance: recovery must never report cost zero for an input the plain parser rejects, which guards the lookahead paths where a vacuous reading is cheapest, and must report exactly the known minimal cost on six probes over lookahead grammars.

\subsection{Relation to prior recovery approaches}
\label{sec:relatedrecovery}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The minimum-cost objective is the classical one of Aho and Peterson~\cite{aho1972} and Lyon~\cite{lyon1974}, who realize it by augmenting a context-free grammar with error productions and parsing every input, valid or not, with an Earley-style algorithm in cubic time; their repaired parse also contains invented tokens. The present design prices deletions and absences inside a descent that runs only after the plain linear-time parse has rejected the input, never invents text, and terminates at the first budget that explains the document. Burke and Fisher~\cite{burke1987} and CPCT+~\cite{diekmann2020} repair over the parser's configuration at the failure point, at token granularity, for LL/LR automata; readings compete over the whole document here, so a repair may lie arbitrarily far before the failure frontier, as the unclosed-quote family of Section~\ref{sec:whyhard} requires, and character granularity suits scannerless PEG parsing, where no token stream exists. The labeled-failure recovery of Medeiros and Mascarenhas~\cite{medeiros2018,medeiros2020} and the permissive grammars of de Jonge et al.~\cite{dejonge2012} modify the grammar, by annotation or automatically, and characterize the recovered parse by the added rules' behavior; here the grammar is untouched and the recovered parse is characterized by Definition~\ref{def:recovered} and the seven-key comparison alone. Swierstra and Duponcheel's error-correcting combinators~\cite{swierstra1996} insert and delete tokens greedily during the parse itself, which cannot weigh a cheap local correction against a better global one; the whole-document judgment exists precisely because the greedy choice is sometimes wrong (Section~\ref{sec:contract}). Tree-sitter~\cite{treesitter} recovers impressively inside a GLR framework with heuristics that carry no stated criterion; the pika parser~\cite{hutchison2020} recovers by virtue of bottom-up parsing at a large always-on constant factor. What is new here is the combination: an unmodified, memoizing, left-recursion-capable PEG parser as the zero-cost core of a priced descent whose output is a complete tree over the damaged input, with a stated cost model, a deterministic judgment, and structural guarantees checked outside the quality metric.

\section{Reference implementation}
\label{sec:software}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

An MIT-licensed reference implementation of the squirrel parser and the recovery engine is available at \url{https://github.com/lukehutch/squirrelparser}. The Dart implementation comprises the parser core, a direct transcription of Fig.~\ref{fig:Code}; a metagrammar, so grammars are written in the ASCII notation used throughout this paper; automatic conversion of parse trees to abstract syntax trees, with rules prefixed \cd{\~{}} (as \cd{WS} in Fig.~\ref{fig:JsonGrammar}) elided as syntactically transparent; and the recovery engine, a single file transcribing Sections~\ref{sec:contract} through~\ref{sec:root} and Figs.~\ref{fig:RecoveryCode} and~\ref{fig:RecoveryCodeSlots}, which consumes the parser core's grammar objects directly, so recovered trees are labeled with the caller's own grammar, carries its own copy of the plain parsing algorithm, and leaves the parser core untouched. The repository also carries the measurement harness behind Sections~\ref{sec:complexity} and~\ref{sec:evaluation}: the mutation battery with its frozen-expectation scoring, the four hard checks, and the performance probes, alongside the parser core's test suite.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Three metadata fields on each memo entry of a packrat parser, a recursion-path flag, a found-recursion flag, and a version stamp validated against a per-position counter, suffice to parse every form of left recursion bottom-up from the fixed point of each cycle, reducing exactly to packrat parsing when no cycle is met (Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction}), remaining linear whenever expansion regions are disjoint (Corollary~\ref{cor:linear}), and degrading, like the prior seed-growing approaches, to quadratic on an adversarial nesting of lookahead over left recursion that we exhibit and measure (Table~\ref{tab:adversarial}). Error recovery then costs one more descent: the same recursion, over priced readings instead of matches, with the same three memo fields governing left recursion in both, the plain parser serving every spent-out query unmodified, and a seven-key judgment selecting one complete tree over the damaged input at minimum cost (Definition~\ref{def:recovered}). Refusing to invent text is what Theorem~\ref{thm:hardness} exacts for tractability, the set-valued repair cells are what Theorem~\ref{thm:frontier} requires of any efficient compositional search, and the measured result, 85.8\% exact structural restoration over 2{,}101 mutants of three grammars at a third of a millisecond each (Table~\ref{tab:battery}), with hard structural checks passed outside the metric, is what the fifty-year literature of in-parser recovery heuristics does not offer for recursive descent parsing.

Three open problems follow directly. First, each budget round recomputes repair cells the previous round already filled; the version mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:purity} extends naturally to reuse across rounds, in the spirit of incremental parsing for LR~\cite{larcheveque1995} and for packrat parsers~\cite{dubroy2017}, and should reduce a multi-error document's cost toward a single priced descent. Second, the judgment's tie rules resolve genuinely ambiguous damage deterministically but blindly; surfacing the tied readings to the client, ranked by the same seven keys, would let tooling present alternatives where the input cannot decide. Third, an input-independent characterization of the grammars on which left-recursive expansion is super-linear (Section~\ref{sec:expansioncost}) remains open, as does the older question of whether PEGs recognize all context-free languages~\cite{ford2004}.
